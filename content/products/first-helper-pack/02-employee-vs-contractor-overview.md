# Employee (W-2) vs. Contractor (1099) — Plain-Language Overview

> **Not legal or tax advice.** This overview is to help you have an informed conversation with your accountant. Misclassifying a worker can lead to back taxes, penalties, and claims. Rules differ between federal (IRS, Department of Labor) and state tests, and some states use stricter tests than others.

---

## The short version

For a typical helper who works **your** schedule, on **your** jobs, using **your** equipment and methods, and doesn't run their own business — **the helper is very likely an employee.** Paying them "on a 1099" doesn't change that.

## What agencies generally look at

| Factor | Points toward **employee** | Points toward **contractor** |
|--------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Control** | You set hours, tell them how to do the work, train them | They decide how, when, and where to do the work |
| **Tools & equipment** | You provide them | They bring their own |
| **Financial risk** | Paid by the hour; no chance of profit or loss | Set their own prices; can make or lose money |
| **Own business** | Works only for you; no business of their own | Has other clients, their own license, insurance, advertising |
| **Relationship** | Ongoing, indefinite | Specific project with a defined end |
| **Core work** | Does the same work your business sells | Provides a separate specialty service |

Some states apply an **"ABC test"** that presumes workers are employees unless the business proves all three: (A) free from control, (B) work is outside the usual course of the business, and (C) the worker is independently established in that trade. Under that kind of test, a helper doing your core service is almost always an employee.

## What changes if they're an employee

- **Payroll taxes:** you withhold income tax and the employee's share of Social Security/Medicare, and pay the employer share (7.65% federal FICA) plus federal and state unemployment taxes.
- **Workers' compensation insurance:** required in most states once you have employees (rules on minimum headcount vary).
- **Minimum wage and overtime:** federal and state minimum wage; overtime for non-exempt employees (typically over 40 hours/week; some states differ).
- **Paperwork:** Form I-9 (employment eligibility), Form W-4, state new-hire reporting, state withholding forms.
- **Other state rules:** paid sick leave, pay frequency, final paycheck timing, required posters.

A **payroll service** handles most of the tax calculations and filings for a monthly fee — usually worth it for a first hire.

## When a contractor might be legitimate

- A licensed specialist you sub a specific task to (e.g., an electrician), who has their own business, insurance, and other clients.
- A fellow operator you split a big job with, where each of you runs your own business.

Get a written subcontract agreement and a certificate of insurance from them.

## Questions to bring to your accountant

1. Based on how I plan to use this helper, should they be W-2 or could they be a contractor in our state?
2. What will my total employer cost be per hour, including state unemployment and workers' comp?
3. Which payroll service do you recommend for one employee?
4. What state registrations do I need before the first paycheck?
5. Do I need workers' comp for one part-time employee here? Roughly what will it cost for my trade class?
6. How should I handle mileage if they drive their own car to jobs?
7. Are there state paid-sick-leave or pay-frequency rules I need to know?
8. Should I change my business structure or insurance before hiring?
