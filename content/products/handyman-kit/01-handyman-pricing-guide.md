# Handyman Pricing Guide

Handyman work loses money in three places: tiny jobs that cost a full trip, "quick" tasks that take three times longer, and the unpaid hour spent at the hardware store. This pricing structure closes all three.

---

## 1. Your hourly target

Open **Your Numbers** in the calculator. With the defaults ($65k take-home, $20k costs, 28 billable hours × 48 weeks) the target is about **$91/hour**. Handyman billable hours are low because of supply runs, estimates, and drive time — that's why the rate is higher than a W-2 tradesperson's wage.

## 2. The pricing structure (recommended)

| Component | Default | Why |
|-----------|---------|-----|
| **Minimum visit charge** | $150 (covers first hour) | A 20-minute job still costs you a drive, setup, and a slot in your day |
| **Task-based price** | Typical hours × rate | Clients compare flat prices; you keep the gain when you get faster |
| **Hourly after scope** | Your target rate, billed in 30-min increments | For undefined work and change orders |
| **Materials** | Receipt + 15–25% markup | Covers pickup time, returns, waste, and the risk of wrong parts |
| **Supply-run charge** | $25–40 per trip | Only when you buy materials for the client |
| **Haul-away** | $40–90 per small load | Dump fees and your time |

## 3. Flat task pricing vs. hourly

**Use flat task prices** when the work is well-defined and you've done it many times (TV mounts, faucet swaps, furniture assembly). Clients like a known number.

**Use hourly with a not-to-exceed estimate** when you can't see the problem (leaks behind walls, "the door sticks sometimes," rotted trim that may go deeper).

> "I charge $[rate]/hour for diagnosis and repair with a one-hour minimum. For this one I estimate 2–3 hours. If it's going to go over 3 hours, I'll stop and check with you first."

## 4. Build a "honey-do list" visit

Most profitable handyman visits bundle 3–6 small tasks into a half day. Price it with the Quote Builder (tasks + quantities), and sell the half day:

> "Put together everything on your list — I'll quote it as one visit. Most lists fit in a 4-hour block."

Half-day and full-day blocks (e.g., 4 hours for [4 × rate − 5%]) are a good offer for repeat clients, as long as the client provides the list in advance.

## 5. Hidden time to price in

Add time for these every time — they're what makes "quick" jobs lose money:
- **Old-house factor:** out-of-plumb walls, plaster, seized fittings, non-standard sizes (+25–50%)
- **Access:** tall ceilings, crawlspaces, attics, furniture to move
- **Drywall and paint:** mud needs dry time — often a second visit
- **Cleanup:** protecting floors, vacuuming dust, hauling packaging
- **Client-supplied parts:** wrong size or missing hardware — state that a return trip is billable

## 6. Materials policy (say it up front)

- **You buy:** receipt cost + [20]% markup + supply-run charge.
- **Client buys:** fine, but if parts are wrong or missing, the extra trip is billable, and you don't warranty client-supplied parts.
- **Big-ticket items** (vanities, doors, fixtures over $200): client buys directly or pays a materials deposit up front.

## 7. Deposits

- Jobs under $500: no deposit; payment on completion.
- Jobs over $500 or with special-order materials: 30–50% deposit, or materials cost up front. (Some states limit deposit size for home improvement work — check yours.)

## 8. When to say no

- The job exceeds your state's license limits.
- It requires permits you can't pull.
- The client wants you to "just cover up" a structural, electrical, or water problem.
- Effective hourly on the quote is well below your target and the client won't adjust scope.
